ECG image classification · 2024

PhysioNet Challenge 2024

Heart Institute (InCor), Hospital das Clínicas, University of São Paulo Medical School

ECG image research at InCor

In 2024, I was already researching ECG image classification for my PhD at the University of São Paulo, working at InCor, a cardiology hospital. Many hospital ECGs were stored as images, while most available models expected the signal as a time series.

The challenge addressed the same problem. We used my doctoral experiments to build team AIMED’s entry and test the model on data we had not seen. We finished first in the classification task.

What is the PhysioNet Challenge?

Since 2000, PhysioNet and Computing in Cardiology have hosted annual algorithm challenges addressing clinical and physiological problems. Shared data, evaluation rules and open-source code allow teams to compare their approaches under common conditions.

The 2024 problem: ECGs on paper

The 2024 edition, Digitization and Classification of ECG Images, had two tasks with separate awards. Teams could enter either task or both:

Digitization
Reconstruct the lead time series from an image of the ECG.
Classification
Identify ECG categories from the image, either directly or using reconstructed signals.

Teams submitted their code. The organizers ran training and evaluation, and each team selected a final entry for hidden-data testing. We focused on direct image classification.

Why we chose to classify the image

Digitization requires locating each lead, separating the trace from the grid, recovering time and amplitude scales, and handling overlap, perspective, shadows and noise. Reconstruction errors also affect signal classification.

We chose to work only on classification. I had already been testing CNNs on ECG images in my PhD, so we used that approach to identify categories directly from the image, without reconstructing the signal.

Doctoral experiments

My research combined synthetic images rendered from public ECG signals with real outpatient and emergency ECG images from InCor. The thesis experiments compared ConvNeXt, EfficientNet-B3, ResNet50, ViT and Swin Transformer, as well as different training strategies.

  • Data augmentation and changes in image resolution.
  • ImageNet initialization, ECG pretraining and the combination of both.
  • Age and sex metadata, and a loss combining BCE and MCC.
  • Ablation studies to isolate the effect of each choice, plus evaluations on photographed and scanned exams.

These experiments belong to the doctoral research. The challenge entry had its own adaptations, described below and in the 2024 paper.

Pretraining

We started with ImageNet weights, then trained the network on synthetic and real ECG images. This stage adapted the representations learned from general photographs to ECG traces before fine-tuning for the challenge.

In my thesis, I also tested starting with random weights and pretraining only on ECGs. Macro F1 was below the ImageNet baseline. ImageNet followed by ECG pretraining performed best among the three configurations below, and we used it in the competition.

Initialization and pretrainingMacro F1
ImageNet, without ECG-specific pretraining0.737
Random weights + ECG pretraining0.565
ImageNet + ECG pretraining0.759

Thesis ablation experiments: 10-fold cross-validation on InCor-EMG. These values are not PhysioNet Challenge results. Doctoral thesis, including Appendix A on the challenge.

Published method

In Image-Based Electrocardiogram Classification Using Pre-trained ConvNext, we describe four training stages:

  1. ImageNet

    We started with a ConvNeXt pretrained on general images.

  2. CODE15 and InCor-AMB

    We adapted the network to synthetic images generated with ECG-Image-Kit and real outpatient ECGs.

  3. InCor-EMG

    We performed an additional fine-tuning stage on emergency ECG images.

  4. PTB-XL

    We fine-tuned for the 11 challenge categories using 5-fold cross-validation and combined the five models into an ensemble.

Training used grayscale images, 224 × 224 resolution for pretraining and 384 × 384 for final fine-tuning, data augmentation, AdamW, class-weighted BCE and early stopping. The 11 categories covered normal ECGs, infarction, ST/T changes, conduction disturbances and arrhythmias.

We excluded CINC21 from pretraining because it contains PTB-XL. We also reduced cross-validation from 10 to 5 folds to fit the challenge computing constraints.

First place: team AIMED

AIMED ranked first among the 16 official classification entries, with a macro F1 of 0.817. The top three results published by the organizers were:

RankTeamRanking macro F1
1stAIMED0.817
2ndInventec AIC0.742
3rdBAPORLab0.730

View the official classification score table

The ranking used color and black-and-white scans of paper ECGs. An extended evaluation included phone photos, stains, deterioration and monitor captures. Our average across those eight scenarios was 0.733. Performance was lower on photographs.

Team

I was an AIMED team member and first author of the paper, alongside Estela Ribeiro, Quenaz B. Soares, José E. Krieger and Marco A. Gutierrez. The work was developed at InCor’s Biomedical Informatics Laboratory, with support from Foxconn Brazil and the Zerbini Foundation.
