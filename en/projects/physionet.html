ECG image classification · 2024

PhysioNet Challenge 2024

Heart Institute (InCor), Hospital das Clínicas, University of São Paulo Medical School

ECG image research at InCor

When the 2024 challenge topic was announced, I was already working with ECG images during my PhD at the University of São Paulo and InCor. Many hospital ECGs were stored as images, while most models expected the signal as a time series. That was the problem I had been studying.

I led team AIMED in the challenge. We used my doctoral experiments to prepare the model and evaluate it on data we had not seen. We placed first in the classification task.

What is the PhysioNet Challenge?

The PhysioNet Challenge is an annual research competition organized by PhysioNet and Computing in Cardiology since 2000. Each year, teams receive a clinical or physiological problem and data for developing their algorithms. The organizers set the rules, evaluate the solutions and make code available for other researchers to study and reproduce the work.

The 2024 problem: ECGs on paper

The 2024 edition, Digitization and Classification of ECG Images, had two tasks with separate awards. Teams could enter either task or both:

Digitization
Reconstruct the lead time series from an image of the ECG.
Classification
Identify ECG categories from the image, either directly or using reconstructed signals.

Teams submitted their code. The organizers ran training and evaluation, and each team selected a final entry for hidden-data testing. We focused on direct image classification.

Why we chose to classify the image

Digitizing an ECG takes more than finding the trace. Each lead must be separated from the grid, time and amplitude scales recovered, and overlap, shadows, perspective and noise handled. An error at this stage can affect the classifier receiving the reconstructed signal.

Since I had already been testing CNNs on ECG images, we focused on direct classification. The model receives the image and identifies the ECG categories without reconstructing the signal first.

Doctoral experiments

During my PhD, I worked with synthetic images rendered from public signals and real ECGs from InCor’s outpatient and emergency departments. I compared ConvNeXt, EfficientNet-B3, ResNet50, ViT and Swin Transformer and tested different ways of training these networks.

  • I tested data augmentation and different image resolutions.
  • I compared ImageNet initialization, ECG pretraining and the combination of both.
  • I experimented with age and sex metadata and a loss combining BCE and MCC.
  • I ran ablation studies to evaluate each choice and tests on photographed and scanned ECGs.

These are thesis experiments. For the challenge, we adapted the training as described below.

Pretraining

We started with ImageNet weights, but an ECG looks very different from the photographs in that dataset. Before fine-tuning for the challenge, we therefore trained the network on synthetic and real ECG images.

In my thesis, I also tested starting with random weights and pretraining only on ECGs. Macro F1 was lower than with ImageNet initialization. Combining ImageNet and ECG pretraining performed best among the three configurations below. We used that combination in the competition.

Initialization and pretrainingMacro F1
ImageNet, without ECG-specific pretraining0.737
Random weights + ECG pretraining0.565
ImageNet + ECG pretraining0.759

Thesis ablation experiments: 10-fold cross-validation on InCor-EMG. These values are not PhysioNet Challenge results. Doctoral thesis, including Appendix A on the challenge.

Published method

In Image-Based Electrocardiogram Classification Using Pre-trained ConvNext, we describe four training stages:

  1. ImageNet

    We started with a ConvNeXt pretrained on general images.

  2. CODE15 and InCor-AMB

    We adapted the network to synthetic images generated with ECG-Image-Kit and real outpatient ECGs.

  3. InCor-EMG

    We performed an additional fine-tuning stage on emergency ECG images.

  4. PTB-XL

    We fine-tuned for the 11 challenge categories using 5-fold cross-validation and combined the five models into an ensemble.

Training used grayscale images, 224 × 224 resolution for pretraining and 384 × 384 for final fine-tuning, data augmentation, AdamW, class-weighted BCE and early stopping. The 11 categories covered normal ECGs, infarction, ST/T changes, conduction disturbances and arrhythmias.

We excluded CINC21 from pretraining because it contains PTB-XL. We also reduced cross-validation from 10 to 5 folds to fit the challenge computing constraints.

First place: team AIMED

AIMED ranked first among the 16 official classification entries, with a macro F1 of 0.817. The top three results published by the organizers were:

RankTeamRanking macro F1
1stAIMED0.817
2ndInventec AIC0.742
3rdBAPORLab0.730

View the official classification score table

The ranking used color and black-and-white scans of paper ECGs. An extended evaluation included phone photos, stains, deterioration and monitor captures. Our average across those eight scenarios was 0.733. Performance was lower on photographs.

Team
